## 2. EXPLORATORY QUESTIONS

### Guiding Questions:

• What are the main questions driving your research?

• What sub-questions will help you delve deeper into your main research question?

• How can these questions guide your search for information and evidence?

Write at least 10 questions using complete sentences.

### Answer

**Main research question:**

How informed are residents of [name of community] about climate and environmental news, common waste types, and practical ways to keep their homes and surroundings clean?

**Sub-questions:**

1. How often do residents receive or encounter climate and environmental news?
2. Which sources do residents use to learn about climate and environmental issues?
3. In which language do residents prefer to receive and discuss this information?
4. How much do residents trust the climate and environmental information they encounter?
5. Which climate and environmental issues can residents identify as affecting Rwanda or their local area?
6. How accurately can residents distinguish common biodegradable materials from non-biodegradable materials?
7. Do residents understand the difference between biodegradable, recyclable, and compostable materials?
8. What practical ways of keeping homes, streets, and shared spaces clean can residents identify?
9. What waste sorting and disposal practices do residents currently report using?
10. Which waste collection, composting, or recycling services are available to residents in the selected community?
11. What makes it difficult for residents to access, understand, or use reliable environmental information?
12. What makes it difficult for residents to sort or dispose of waste safely and properly?
13. Where do residents seek advice when they have questions about climate, the environment, or waste?
14. What environmental topics do residents say they most need clear information about?

These questions will guide searches for Rwanda-specific evidence and the collection of local survey, interview, and observation data. The research will establish the local findings rather than assume in advance that residents lack information or knowledge.

## 4. EVIDENCE

### Guiding Questions:

• What types of statistical data are necessary to support your research findings?

• Are there significant trends or patterns in the data that you need to identify?

• Which quotes or expert opinions will add credibility and depth to your research?

### Answer

Collect and report the following evidence from residents of **[name of community]**. Record how participants were selected, how many were invited and participated, the question wording, and the denominator for every percentage. Do not report local gaps as facts until local data have been collected.

1. The number of residents invited, participating, and declining, with the areas of the community from which participants came.
2. How often residents encounter climate and environmental news, which sources they use, and which languages they encounter or prefer.
3. Residents’ reported trust in the sources from which they receive environmental information.
4. Scores on a short set of fact-checked questions about climate and environmental issues relevant to Rwanda and the local area.
5. The percentage of common waste items residents correctly identify as biodegradable or non-biodegradable, reported item by item.
6. The percentage of residents who correctly distinguish biodegradability from recyclability and compostability.
7. Residents’ knowledge of practical ways to keep homes, streets, and shared community spaces clean.
8. Residents’ reported household waste sorting, disposal, composting, and litter-prevention practices, using a clearly stated recall period.
9. Verified information about local collection services, disposal options, and recycling or composting routes, including which materials each accepts where this is known.
10. The number and percentage of residents reporting barriers to receiving reliable information or following safe waste practices.
11. Counts of visible litter or waste at selected public locations, recorded using the same observation method and clearly stated boundaries.
12. Short, consented comments from residents and relevant local environmental or waste-management representatives about information needs, waste knowledge, and local barriers. Use exact quotations only when recorded accurately and permission has been given.

Look for patterns by information source, language, type of waste item, reported practice, and access to local services. Compare groups only where participant numbers allow a fair comparison, and protect residents’ privacy. Use national or district statistics as background, not as a substitute for the local findings.

## 5. RISKS & CHALLENGES

### Guiding Questions:

• What potential risks or challenges might you face during your research?

• How will you mitigate or address these risks and challenges?

• What backup plans do you have if things do not go as expected?

### Answer

| Risk or challenge | Mitigation or backup plan |
|---|---|
| Residents may refuse to participate or may be reluctant to share information. | Explain the purpose clearly, make participation voluntary, allow people to skip questions, and do not pressure anyone. If people decline, record the number of refusals without recording their names. |
| Residents may fear that their answers could cause trouble or affect their access to local services. | Explain that responses are confidential and will not affect services. Where possible, speak with residents privately and keep community leaders from viewing individual answers. |
| Participants may give inaccurate or exaggerated information, or answers they think the researcher wants to hear. | Use neutral wording and a stated recall period. Compare self-reported practices with knowledge questions and carefully defined observations; identify self-reported results as such. |
| Participants may repeat false or unverified climate or environmental claims. | Record the claim as a participant’s view, not established fact. Check factual claims against authoritative sources such as REMA, NISR, and IPCC publications. |
| Technical terms such as “biodegradable,” “recyclable,” or “compostable” may be misunderstood. | Use simple wording and locally familiar examples. Review definitions with a knowledgeable local source and pilot the questions before the main data collection. |
| Questions may be misunderstood because of language or literacy differences. | Prepare clear Kinyarwanda and English versions, have translations reviewed, read questions aloud when needed, and revise questions that residents interpret differently. |
| Some residents may be absent when researchers visit or may be missed by the recruitment approach. | Visit at more than one suitable time and recruit from different parts of the selected community. Describe who was reached and who may have been missed. |
| Community leaders or gatekeepers may delay access or influence who agrees to take part. | Explain the study and request appropriate access in advance, while inviting residents privately and voluntarily. If access is delayed, use the approved alternative locations or adjust the fieldwork sequence. |
| Some residents may not want to discuss cleanliness, dumping, or household practices because they feel judged. | Use respectful, non-blaming language, avoid asking for names of people who dump waste, and let participants skip sensitive questions. |
| National or district statistics may not represent the selected local community. | Label the geographic coverage, year, and source of every statistic. Collect local baseline data and do not present national figures as local results. |
| Seasonal conditions or a recent community event may affect news exposure, waste, or cleanliness observations. | Record when and where observations take place and note relevant conditions. Use consistent observation locations and methods, and explain these limits in the findings. |
| Residents’ privacy may be compromised by identifiable survey answers, quotations, or photographs. | Obtain informed consent, use participant codes, remove identifying details, and do not publish names or identifiable images without separate permission. |
| The research team may record answers inconsistently or introduce bias. | Train the team with a shared question guide, use the same definitions and recording method, and review a portion of completed records for consistency. |
| Waste observations could expose researchers to sharp, medical, chemical, or otherwise hazardous items. | Observe only from safe public areas and do not handle waste. Stop an observation if the location is unsafe. |
| Forms may be incomplete, lost, or contain contradictory answers. | Review forms for missing entries while still in the field, store completed forms securely, keep a backup record, and report missing data instead of guessing. |
| The available time or resources may be insufficient to reach every part of the community. | Prioritize the main research questions, document the areas and participants reached, and state the limits of the findings rather than making unsupported claims. |
